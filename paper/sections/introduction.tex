\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning with LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora} has made it cheap
to specialize a base model for a task, and organizations now maintain libraries
of adapters layered over a frozen foundation checkpoint. The checkpoint,
however, is not frozen for long. Vendors ship new versions of a family on a
cadence of months (Qwen2 to Qwen2.5, Llama~3.0 to 3.1, Gemma~3 to Gemma~4), and
every adapter trained on the old base must, in current practice, be retrained
on its original task data. That data is often private, expensive to
re-collect, or simply gone, and the cost scales with the number of adapters.
The naive alternative, loading the old adapter onto the new base unchanged, is
known to retain only part of the fine-tuning benefit~\citep{lin2025finetuningtransfer}.

The reason naive transfer degrades is that a LoRA adapter is not a free-standing
object. Its $A$ factor reads coordinates of the base model's activations and its
$B$ factor writes coordinates back (\S\ref{sec:problem}); it is a function
expressed in the old model's representation basis. A version update retrains
the base, and the basis moves. We measure that movement directly
(\S\ref{sec:drift}): across the 96 attention projections of
Qwen2-0.5B\,$\to$\,Qwen2.5-0.5B the input activations of \emph{every} module
differ between versions by more than a rotation, with linear-CKA drift ranging
from $0.09$ in the first layer to $0.69$ at the most-drifted attention-output
projection. Drift is module-specific and grows with depth.

Recent work has recognized that adapters should be portable across bases, and
two training-free methods exist for ordinary LoRA adapters: Fine-tuning
Transfer~\citep{lin2025finetuningtransfer} adds the weight delta unchanged
(the naive baseline), and Cross-LoRA~\citep{xia2025crosslora} projects the delta
onto singular subspaces of the target base weights. Both read the relation
between the two models from their \emph{weights}. Cross-LoRA is motivated by
heterogeneous pairs with mismatched dimensions, and its evaluation uses
cross-family pairs on benchmarks where the retrained adapter itself gains
under one point over the base, so it is not known how much of a large task
gain a weight-space projection preserves under same-family version drift. Our
drift measurement suggests the signal it needs may not be in the weights at
all: a change in the inputs a module receives, produced by every layer
upstream, does not change that module's weight SVD.

This paper takes the activation route. We run a small unlabeled anchor set
(generic text, no task data) through both base versions, cache token-aligned
activations at every adapted module, and solve, in closed form, a pair of
per-module linear maps that translate between the two versions' input and
output geometries. Folding the maps into the LoRA factors gives a transplanted
adapter $\Delta W'=P^{\mathrm{out}}\Delta W\,P^{\mathrm{in}}$ of the same rank
that loads onto the new base with standard tooling and adds no inference cost
(\S\ref{sec:method}). Because the maps are fit by regression rather than
constrained to a subspace or a rotation, they can represent the non-orthogonal
drift we observe; we show on controlled drift that this freedom matters
(\S\ref{sec:ablations}), and on real version pairs we compare directly against
Cross-LoRA, with naive transfer as the lower bound and a retrained oracle as
the upper bound, reporting the fraction of the oracle--naive gap each method
recovers on tasks where that gap is large (\S\ref{sec:experiments}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Version drift as the setting.} We study adapter transfer across
        successive releases of one model family with matching architecture and
        tokenizer, the routine operational case for adapter libraries, which
        isolates representational drift from the dimension and vocabulary
        changes that motivate prior cross-model work (\S\ref{sec:problem},
        \S\ref{sec:related}).
  \item \textbf{A per-module drift study on real checkpoints.} Using linear CKA
        over unlabeled anchor activations, we characterize where and how much
        the activation geometry of LoRA-target modules moves between versions,
        and show that the drift is non-orthogonal (unconstrained maps beat the
        best rotation in every layer), module-specific, largest at the
        attention-output projection, and not organized by attention head: a
        version bump redistributes information across heads rather than
        reordering them (\S\ref{sec:drift}).
  \item \textbf{Activation-grounded closed-form alignment.} We derive the
        rank-preserving transplant and fit its maps by ridge regression or
        orthogonal Procrustes on anchor activations, in seconds, from a single
        forward pass of the anchors through each base (\S\ref{sec:method}).
  \item \textbf{Ablations that weight-space methods lack.} On controlled drift
        we show that unconstrained maps recover substantially more than
        orthogonal ones, that per-module maps are required (a shared map is
        worse than naive transfer), and that roughly one hundred anchor tokens
        suffice at small width; we repeat the key sweeps on the real pair
        (\S\ref{sec:ablations}).
  \item \textbf{A direct comparison with Cross-LoRA on a meaningful scale.} We
        evaluate naive transfer, Cross-LoRA, and our Procrustes and ridge
        variants against a retrained oracle on tasks with a large oracle lift,
        using the fraction of the oracle--naive gap recovered as the metric,
        and release code, anchor sets, and fitted maps (\S\ref{sec:experiments},
        \S\ref{sec:repro}).
\end{itemize}
